Conditions
Land regrowth—

How fast a grazed field comes back. This, more than anything else, sets how many people the world can hold.

Harvest rate—

What one farmer can lift from a field in a day. Raising it does not create food — it just strips the land faster.

Storage efficiency—

How much of a surplus survives being put by. Cheap storage is what lets wealth accumulate at all.

Heritable drift—

How far a child's metabolism strays from its parent's. At zero, nothing evolves.

Gregariousness—

How much company is worth, priced in crop. A grazed cell holds about 0.07, so 0.006 means ten neighbours are worth roughly one cell of food. Wind it up and they clump hard enough to starve.

Property & arms
Rent share—

What a tenant hands over from every load lifted off a lord's ground — before enforcement, which decides how much of it actually arrives.

Improvement—

How much faster owned land regrows. This is the other half of the bargain: set it below the rent and tenancy is simply robbery, and the tenants walk.

Cost of enclosure—

Capital a farmer must have saved before he can fence anything. Lower it and the commons goes quickly.

Cost of a soldier—

Arms and provisions, per man per day, out of the lord's purse. Raise it far enough and no rent can be collected at all.

Who's who

Farmers are coloured by stored capital, not by what is in their belly — one going hungry today shows red whatever the granary holds. Lords and soldiers are coloured by what they are, because on the map that is the thing worth finding. Gold squares are manors and the pale ones are the capitals of states; a red halo means that state is at war. Territory is tinted by state and outlined hard — the thin seams inside a bloc are the manors it is made of.

Classes
Bandpeopleof popof wealth

The bands are absolute, not quantiles: Subsisting is fed, Comfortable can afford a child, Wealthy can afford to enclose ground and Magnate can afford to hold it. A society of identical paupers reads as entirely Destitute — the honest answer, and the one percentile bands would get wrong. These are wealth and nothing else: a soldier and a small freeholder land in the same band on very different terms, which is what the role census below is for.

The map
Groundshare
Actually feeds anyone—

Water is not walkable and grows nothing, but a cell on the shore draws on the shallows next to it — so a coastline feeds people even where the soil behind it is thin, and the same is true of a desert that happens to meet the sea. Rock and dry interior feed nobody at all.

Resourceon mapenclosedvs avg
Oil income—

Four deposits, four separate geographies: ore in the high ground, oil under desert and the offshore shelf, fuel in the forests and coal measures, fertiliser on coastal silt. Each acts through a channel the simulation already had — fertiliser feeds more tenants, ore arms more men cheaply, fuel makes ground cheaper to hold. Oil is the odd one: an income that does not care how many tenants you have, which is exactly why it distorts everything around it. The last column is whether the lords ended up on the good ground or merely on the ground that grew food.

Kept under arms—

A title collects nothing on its own. Rent actually taken is the rent share times enforcement, and enforcement is what the garrison can cover — below that, the difference simply stays in the tenants' hands. The bar sitting under full is a countryside paying less than it is asked for.

Fallen in war—

Every manor is founded sovereign. When two borders touch, the pair has to settle what they are to each other, and there are only two answers: evenly matched neighbours federate, because conquest is expensive; lopsided ones fight, because it is not. Both roads end in a larger state and differ only in the terms — a partner pays a levy, a conquered estate pays tribute. That gap is what makes a war worth starting.

A settlement is a patch of ground holding twice the average density, found by flood fill — nobody places them. Before there was property they formed on good soil, drifted and dissolved, because a farmer has to spread out to eat. A manor is the first thing in this world that gives a crowd a reason to stay put.
